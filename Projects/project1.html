01 - Engineering Process

Objectives and Context

ENPH 253 is UBC Engineering Physics' summer Instrumental Design course, in which teams of 4 take part in an intensive, all-encompasing robotics project working up to a competitive autonomous robotics competition at the end.

Competition Overview

The 2026 competition challenged teams to design, build, and program an autonomous Mars rover capable of navigating an 8 × 8 ft course, including a ramp, while completing as many mission objectives as possible. These mission objectives tested a range of abilities, from mechanical manipulation and structural assembly to autonomous navigation and computer vision. The 5 tasks teams could choose from were as follows:

Mission Objectives

  1. Rock retrieval (2 points): Locate, retrieve, and carry a rock containing aluminum through the course.
  2. Alien identification (1 point per alien): Locate and identify alien figurines (Teletubbies) throughout the course using computer vision, with false positives earning no points.
  3. Radio tower construction (1 point per piece): Assemble a three-piece radio tower from components arranged on the ground into a fully upright structure.
  4. Solar panel recovery (2 points): Locate and uncover a hidden solar panel.
  5. Habitat construction (1 point per piece): Assemble a circular habitat from four separate pieces.

Our team chose to focus on all tasks except the habitat construction, maximizing our potential points at 9

Chassis + Drive Systems Design

Purpose: Provide the framing, containment, and motion of the robot to carry all systems throughout the course.

Design Rules/Limitations:

  • Must never extend beyond 18" in length in any direction
  • Must not purchase over $200 worth of materials (including electrical and software)
  • Must have a switch to enable/disable power from our designated 4s and 2s LiPo batteries

Design Decisions

Drivetrain

The robot is powered by a balanced four-wheel drive system designed around using Mecanum wheels for full 360deg movement control, using practically identical motors and gearboxes to power each of the 4 motors. This design stood out amongst that of our competitors, as majority of the other teams were using an encoded dual rear-wheel drive system with a caster wheel in the front. We opted for this design because we needed optimal space within chassis to support all our systems, but still wanted to be able to turn on the spot to make some of our tasks simpler. As such, meccanum wheels seemed a natural direction to go, and we were excited for the challenge they brought with them. We were initially concerned that the 4-wheel drive system would cause our robot to struggle going up the ramp, as the robot might have been too ridgid or the weight distribution may have caused it to slip. Fortunately due to our choice of motors, neither of these issues were prevalent.

Deadwheel positioning System

However, with the choice of a four-wheel maccanum drive system of course came other challenges. For instance, we were no longer able to use encoded motors, as meccanum wheels rely on a degree of slipping to allow for any motion, making any encoders mounted to them innacurate. This would then also make it difficult for us to navigate the course, as it was standard that the robots follow the line of tape throughout the course, requiring encoders to navigate any distance strayed from the path of the line to accomplish tasks.

I resolved this problem by proposing and designing two mechanical deadwheels, mounted in seperate x and y orientations within the centre of our drive motors. These were free-spinning wheels with rollers perpendicular to their rolling directions, allowing them to translate in all directions without struggle. The deadwheels were mounted in perpendicular directions, one facing our defined "x" direction and one along "y". The goal of these wheels were to mount them with a magnetic encoder and allow them to freely pivot to the ground, so as the robot drives the wheels turn freely and indicate to us how far we have travelled. Surprisingly, this system worked practically flawlessly after the second iteration, and allowed us to completely avoid tape following at all, instead defining a start position on the course and plot out the movement of the robot through defined coordinate points and orientations we instructed it to travel to. As can be seen, the encoders were simply mounted on the side of the carriage, with the magnet being mounted within the spinning axle rod. I then drilled 4 holes through the central frame of the wheel, and designed and printed a PRECISE mount that would slide into a guide slot in the axle and bolt into the side of the wheel, securing the system within the carriage. The axle would then spin on ball bearings, while the carriage itself spun on bearings in the carriage, allowing it to pivot and stay on the ground at all times. A simple press-fit cap was inserted at the other end of the axle to keep it from translating back and forth.

Chassis Design

The chassis of T.A.M.K. was HEAVILY iterated over, though many key aspects remained the same. As can be seen in the segmented view from the carousel above, the chassis was designed to be completely modular, allowing all team members to work with different parts of the chassis at any given time to maximize efficiency. For example, we did ample testing with the front chassis-elevator-leadscrew unit while another team member was drivetrain tuning with the entire rest of the chassis, as we were able to simply remove all electrical and mechanical components by undoing a few bolts.

Front Chassis: The front chassis was designed to house our main manipulator systems. including our elevator claw and lead-spear system (to be touched upon in following sections). This required designing around attatchment points to the baseplate and rear chassis, integrating 2 stepper motors, an entire lead screw slider with sufficient enough room to move, while being restricted enough to slide rather than rotate, adding securment holes for the elevator unit, and adding gaps and internal pathways to route wiring from all the motor systems and 3 integrated limit switches up to our ESP boards.

Rear Chassis The rear chassis is comprised of 5 components: The drive-chassis, three wiring plate frame layers, and the top chassis/rock and battery housing unit.

  • Drive-chassis: Minimal design considerations were needed for the drive chassis outside of attatchment points to the front chassis, wheelwell depths/motor interface locations, and bolt holes to support an internal hanging wiring frame to house our H-bridge and metal detecting units.
  • Wiring Plates/Frames: 3 Wiring plates and their respective frames were included in the design for the robot. the main purpose of these plates were to organize and designate specific slots for our PCB units, as well as providing easy plug-in access to our ESP boards and slots to include our flip-switches. The wiring plates themselves also included holes to other layers, allowing for cross-layer integration between circuit boards being entirely internalized within the robot.
  • Top-chassis: The top chassis was designed to sit nearly flush with the elevator claw when tilted back, acting as a landing place for the claw to flip over and dump the aluminium rock, allowing us to easily carry it throughout the course on top of the robot. Additionally, I designed our LiPo batteries to sit on the exterior of the top-chassis, allowing us easy access to unplug them in the scenario they became unsafe.
Additionally, we designed our robot chassis to fit within an 11"x11" area, well within our 18" confinement. This was done to provide me with additional room, as I had an idea to "cheat" the solar pannel task by simply including a rigid "solar claw" that extended out the right side of our chassis, and would be angled and positioned procisely to be aligned with the loop of the solar pannel, allowing us to complete the solar pannel task by simply running our robot into the side of the solar pannel, then driving straight and allowing the claw to loop the cover and rip it off. I dubbed the final version of this claw "EXODUS", named after the mass exodus of 6 tipped spike ends that protrude from it, as well as its goal of removing the pannel cover.

Design Decisions

Lead-Screw System (Name: The Spear)

This system was designed to overcome our main objective: Assembling the radio tower. The radio tower task, as can be seen in the course layout, was comprised of 3 parts: picking up the tower pieces, assembling the tower, and placing the tower on the extruded aluminium box. The cylindrical tower pieces were laid out in a uniform plate FLAT on the ground, with their hollow insides pointing towards the course. There was then a piece of tape laid out from the main navigation tape line to the center of the layout plate, and a second piece of tape laid out from the navigation tape out towards the metal

Our plan? To align our robot with that central piece of tape, then assemble the tower ON the robot while it was completely stationary, then drive to the boss, center ourselves using a centering crescent, and simply lower the tower down onto the boss. This would make the task substantially easier, as our robot would never have to move to realign itself with the towers that were not lined up along the central tape!   • The Lead Stepper: Ordered off amazon, we used a Nema17 1.7A 40Ncm stepper motor with a 300mm T8 lead screw and bearing attatched to it. Various design considerations were CRUCIAL to this system, such as the 300mm lead screw, as we needed the lead screw to span 164mm (the length that would reach all 3 tower pieces). Additionally, consideration towards the stepper, where it was to sit, and how we were to get a control wire to it was all considered and designed around. The culminated in the stepper being PERFECTLY fitted into the left side of the chassis, with a gap at the top for the control wire to route directly to the stepper easily. Additionally, the opposite end of the lead screw was mounted inside a ball bearing, which would be perfectly pressfit into a gap at the other end of the front chassis.
