Project 01

Robot Summer Autonomous Mars Rover (T.A.M.K.)

Mechanical Lead on a four-person team tasked with designing, building, and competing in UBC ENPH 253's summer robotics competition. I designed and fabricated most of the robot's mechanical systems, documented our approach in the required design proposal, and contributed to electrical integration, assembly, and testing.

My role: Mechanical Lead

Project period: May 2026 – August 2026

Team: 4 people

Technical Overview

Skills Applied

Mechanical Design CAD (Onshape) Machining Waterjet Cutting Laser Cutting 3D Printing Rapid Prototyping Mechanical Assembly Soldering Crimping Electrical Integration Troubleshooting Testing & Iteration

01 — Engineering Process

Objectives and Context

ENPH 253 is UBC Engineering Physics' summer Instrumental Design course. Teams of four take on an intensive robotics project, progressing from design and fabrication through electrical integration and software development before competing in an autonomous robotics competition.

Competition Overview

The 2026 competition challenged us to design, build, and program an autonomous Mars rover capable of navigating an 8 × 8 ft course, including a ramp, while completing as many mission objectives as possible. The tasks tested mechanical manipulation, structural assembly, autonomous navigation, and computer vision.

Mission Objectives

  1. Rock retrieval (2 points): Locate, retrieve, and carry a rock containing aluminium foil through the course.
  2. Alien identification (1 point per alien): Locate and identify Teletubby figurines using computer vision. False positives earned no points for aliens that round.
  3. Radio tower construction (1 point per piece): Assemble a three-piece radio tower from components arranged on the ground onto an aluminium extrusion.
  4. Solar-panel recovery (2 points): Remove the cover from a solar panel positioned at a designated location on the course.
  5. Habitat construction (1 point per piece): Assemble a circular habitat from four separate pieces.

Our team chose to pursue every task except habitat construction, giving us a potential total of 9 points. That meant fitting several different mechanisms into a compact robot while keeping them accessible, serviceable, and compatible with one another.

02 — Engineering Process

Engineering Design

As Mechanical Lead, I was responsible for designing and fabricating most of T.A.M.K.'s mechanical systems, including the chassis, drivetrain, lead-screw mechanism, deadwheel positioning system, and several components of the chain-lift elevator. I also contributed to protoboard assembly and soldering, while taking primary responsibility for electrical integration, including PCB placement, wiring connections, and cable routing.

I also had to document our approach in the required design proposal, explaining our chosen mechanisms and how they addressed the competition's constraints. Throughout the project, I had to consider not only how each mechanism would work on its own, but how it would fit within the robot and work alongside everything else.

Chassis & Drivetrain

Purpose

Provide a compact structure for the robot's mechanical and electrical systems while allowing it to manoeuvre reliably throughout the course.

Design Constraints

  • Remain within the competition's 18-inch size limit.
  • Stay within the $200 materials budget, including electrical and software components.
  • Include switches to enable and disable power from the designated 4S and 2S LiPo batteries.

Drivetrain Design

We chose a four-wheel Mecanum drivetrain for our robot as opposed to the 2-wheel drive system many of our classmates went for. We opted for this design to maximize useable space within chassis to support all our systems, while retaining the ability to turn on the spot to make some of our tasks simpler. This gave us more flexibility when manoeuvring within the confined course and lining up with the mission objectives.

However, with the choice of a four-wheel maccanum drive system of course came other challenges. In particular, we were no longer able to use encoded motors, as meccanum wheels rely on a degree of slipping to allow for any motion, making any encoders mounted to them innacurate. This would then also make it difficult for us to navigate the course, as it was standard that the robots follow the line of tape throughout the course, requiring encoders to navigate any distance strayed from the path of the line to accomplish tasks.

We were also initially concerned that the four-wheel drive might struggle on the ramp because of traction, rigidity, or weight distribution. Fortunately, our motor selection performed well during testing, and the ramp did not become the limitation we had anticipated.

Deadwheel Positioning System

Purpose

Measure the robot's displacement independently of its drive wheels, allowing us to navigate using coordinates rather than continuously following the course tape.

Why Deadwheels?

Because the Mecanum rollers can slip, the drive encoders could not reliably tell us how far the robot had actually travelled. We also found that PID tuning and tape-following were difficult to run at a reasonable speed with our relatively heavy robot. I proposed using deadwheels to measure actual ground displacement, giving us a more direct way to navigate between programmed coordinates without depending on continuous tape following reading.

1 / 3

Wheel Arrangement and Measurement

I designed two independent deadwheels mounted perpendicular to one another to measure displacement along our defined X and Y axes. Each wheel's rolling direction measured one axis, while its perpendicular rollers allowed it to accommodate movement in other directions without binding against the ground.

For proper navigation, consistent ground contact important. The deadwheel needed to stay against the floor so its encoder would measure real movement rather than lose contact over small changes in the surface. At the same time, the assembly had to pivot freely without creating unnecessary drag or placing excessive load on the wheel and its mounting hardware. To accomplish this, I simply allowed the deadwheel carriage to pivot freely, allowing the natural weight of the deadwheel and its carriage to keep it rolling. This worked exceptionally well.

Axle and Encoder Arrangement

I designed the assembly around a rotating axle with a magnet positioned for the magnetic encoder. The axle rotated on ball bearings, while a separate bearing-supported carriage could pivot to keep the wheel in contact with the ground. This separated the wheel's rotation from the carriage's movement and allowed the encoder to measure wheel rotation consistently.

Testing and Result

After two iterations, the system worked practically flawlessly. It gave us a much more useful way to navigate by starting position, coordinates, and orientation, while letting us move faster than we could reasonably manage with tape following alone.

Modular Chassis Design

I wanted the chassis to make development easier, not just hold everything together. Several mechanisms needed to be designed, fabricated, and tested at the same time, so I made the front manipulator assembly detachable from the rear chassis using only a few bolts. This let us separate the major assemblies without repeatedly dismantling the entire robot.

It was especially useful during development: I could work on the front chassis, elevator, and lead-screw mechanism while another teammate tuned the drivetrain. We could also test the front mechanisms separately and then bring them back together for integration. The chassis went through iteration, but keeping this modularity was a deliberate design decision throughout the process.

1 / 4

Front Chassis

I designed the front chassis around the elevator, lead-screw carriage, two stepper motors, and their mounting hardware. The layout needed to leave both mechanisms enough room to move while accounting for attachment points, protruding fasteners, limit switches, and internal wiring paths to the ESP boards. These considerations shaped the chassis geometry as much as the mechanisms themselves.

Rear Chassis

The rear assembly was divided into the drive chassis, three wiring-plate and frame layers, and a top chassis. Each layer had a specific purpose, while the overall arrangement kept the electronics accessible and the robot's mechanisms compact.

  • Drive chassis: I designed the base around the front-chassis attachment points, wheel-well depths, and motor interfaces. It also included bolt holes for the internal frame supporting the H-bridge and metal-detection hardware.
  • Three wiring-plate and frame layers: These layers organized the PCBs and provided slots for mounting them, access to the ESP boards, and slots for the power switches. I also included holes between layers so wiring could pass through the chassis and connect the internal PCBs without needing to route every connection around the outside.
  • Top chassis: I positioned the top chassis nearly flush with the elevator claw when it tilted backward, giving the claw a surface to deposit the recovered aluminium rock. The LiPo batteries were mounted externally for easy access and disconnection when needed.

EXODUS — Solar-Panel Cover Removal

We kept the main chassis to approximately 11 × 11 inches, well inside the competition's 18-inch limit. This was also important for accommodating EXODUS, my passive solar-panel cover-removal mechanism. I wanted to complete the task without adding another powered manipulator or increasing the mechanical complexity of the robot.

I designed EXODUS as a rigid claw with six protruding spike tips. By positioning the tips to catch the cover's loop, the robot could pull the cover away simply by driving past it. The mechanism relied on the robot's movement rather than a separate actuator.

Although an IR beacon sat behind the solar panel, we planned to locate it using our absolute-positioning system rather than beacon tracking.

Lead-Screw System — “The Spear”

Purpose and Assembly Approach

The Spear was designed to retrieve and assemble the three radio-tower pieces on the stationary robot before placing the completed tower onto a raised mounting boss. The pieces were arranged flat on the ground, with their hollow openings facing towards the course. Our plan was to line up with the central approach tape and assemble the tower without moving the chassis between pickups.

Once the tower was assembled, we could drive to the mounting boss. The centring crescent would guide the boss underneath the tower, after which the Spear could withdraw and the elevator could lower the tower into place. This meant each mechanism had to work together as part of one repeatable sequence.

Stepper Motor & Lead Screw

I used a NEMA 17 stepper rated at 1.7 A and 40 N·cm holding torque, paired with a 300 mm T8 lead screw. The mechanism needed approximately 164 mm of travel to reach all three tower pieces while still fitting within the front chassis.

I integrated the stepper on the left side of the chassis, leaving a gap for its control wiring. The opposite end of the lead screw was supported by a bearing press-fitted into the front chassis. The carriage was constrained against rotation but allowed to translate along the lead screw.

The motor needed enough torque to move the carriage against friction and mechanical drag without skipping steps. Since the carriage was constrained from rotating or moving freely in other directions, the lead screw had to turn that motor rotation into controlled linear travel rather than force the stepper to lose position.

1 / 3

Spear Chassis & Sliding Carriage

I designed the sliding carriage in Onshape around the available space and the hardware already installed in the chassis. Bolts and tape-following components protruded into its path, so I had to account for their positions while maintaining enough constraint to prevent the carriage from rotating.

The first fit was too tight and caused binding. I adjusted the clearance to allow smoother translation without introducing excessive play. This was a good example of how the dimensions that looked right in CAD still needed iteration once the part was fabricated and fitted alongside real hardware.

The servo was mounted directly to the moving carriage using two M2 bolts and nuts. It actuated the spear, and its positioning also allowed it to trigger the home limit switch. Keeping the servo on the carriage meant it travelled with the mechanism rather than requiring a separate stationary linkage.

Spear Unit

The spear needed to enter the hollow tower pieces, collect them, and help hold them in position during assembly. I designed a thin leading section to fit through the tower-piece openings, transitioning into a wider, chamfered section that guided the pieces into place and helped prevent unwanted movement.

I tested the pickup and assembly sequence to check that the spear could return to a consistent position for each piece. Repeatable pickups mattered because the robot was intended to assemble the tower while stationary, without having to reposition itself for every component.

Centring Crescent

Finally, I designed a rigid centring crescent at the rear of the Spear. Its circular profile matched the diameter of the mounting boss and was concentric with the tower's position on the mechanism. As the robot approached the boss, the crescent was intended to guide the boss underneath the assembled tower.

Once the tower was centred over the boss, the Spear could withdraw and the elevator could lower the tower into place. This gave us a mechanical way to correct the final alignment rather than relying entirely on precise driving.

Chain-Lift System — “The Elevator”

Purpose

Lift and manipulate objects at the front of the robot, including retrieving the aluminium rock and working alongside The Spear to assemble and place the radio tower.

Design Approach

We chose a chain lift instead of a three-degree-of- freedom arm because space at the front of the robot was limited, and we wanted to avoid the added mechanical and programming complexity. Compared with a belt or drawer-slide arrangement, the chain also let the elevator tilt and flip backwards. That meant the same mechanism could retrieve the rock and dump it into the top chassis, as well as lift and assemble the radio-tower pieces alongside The Spear.

My teammate designed the claw, tower pieces, and mounting mechanism. I designed the sprockets, chain-to-claw mounting link, and stepper-motor integration. We then built and iterated on the overall assembly together.

1 / 4

Sprocket Design & Fabrication

Since bicycle chain was available in the lab, I designed custom sprockets for waterjet cutting rather than sourcing a new drive system. I measured the chain and accounted for its pitch, tooth spacing, tooth profile, and slope angles so the links would engage correctly. I also incorporated the D-shaft interface needed to drive the sprockets.

To secure the sprockets in place, I machined fourteen shaft collars using the lab lathe and drill. This is just one example where I had to make hardware to suit the assembly rather than relying entirely on off-the-shelf components.

Chain-to-Claw Mounting Link

I adapted the existing chain-link geometry by adding an external plate with mounting holes for the claw. The modified link still needed to fit into the chain and engage with the sprockets, so preserving the original pitch and engagement geometry was essential.

Stepper Integration

I designed the elevator stepper's mounting section and integrated it into the front chassis. Since the two stepper motors were relatively heavy, I positioned the elevator motor opposite the lead-screw motor to improve horizontal weight distribution.

Unfortunately, though the elevator mechanism and tower assembly sequence was functional, a power surge during calibration the night before competition prevented us from fully integrating the tower-assembly sequence into the competition program.

03 — Engineering Process

Fabrication & Integration

Turning our CAD designs into a working robot meant making parts that fitted together accurately, assembling several moving mechanisms in a limited space, and making sure the electrical hardware could be installed without getting in the way. The details that worked in CAD still had to work with real hardware, tolerances, and tools.

I worked across several fabrication methods, choosing the process based on the component: waterjet cutting for the custom chain sprockets, lathe and drill-press work for the shaft collars, and 3D printing for custom mounting components. I also used hand tools for assembly, adjustments, and integrating the separate mechanisms.

From CAD to Assembly

Meccanum Wheel Part Fabrication

though the meccanum wheels were provided through the lab, I designed and printed each of their motor mounts to bind them to the base plate, and even had to fabricate a hex-shaped shaft collar for one of the wheels that was missing one on the lathe, drill press, and mill. I did this by press fitting a brass standoff into a lathed aluminum collar, then creating a set screw through the drill press and manual tapping. This shaft ended up working incredibly well.

Methods/Tools Used:

Primary - Machining (Lathe, Drill Press, Mill) Secondary - 3D printing Assembly - Press fit, Setscrews, Bolts

Deadwheel Fabrication

The deadwheel assembly required the axle to rotate freely on its bearings while keeping the encoder magnet aligned with the sensor. I drilled four mounting holes through the central wheel frame and designed a precise 3D-printed encoder mount. The mount slid into a guide on the axle and bolted to the wheel, holding the sensor in the correct position relative to the magnet. A press-fit cap retained the axle against axial movement without restricting rotation, while separate bearings allowed the carriage to pivot.

Methods/Tools Used:

Primary - 3D printing Secondary - Drill Press Assembly - Press fit, Bolts

Lead-Screw Assembly

For The Spear, I had to account for clearances around bolt heads and the tape-following hardware that protruded into the carriage's path. The lead screw also needed support at its far end, so I designed the chassis to accept a press-fit bearing. The servo mount used two M2 bolts and nuts to secure the servo to the moving carriage, which had a press-fit lead-screw bearing that bolted through it with 4 M3 bolts. After the first fit bound, I adjusted the carriage clearance to allow smooth translation while maintaining alignment.

Methods/Tools Used:

Primary - 3D printing Assembly - Press fit, Bolts

Chassis Fabrication and Assembly

The chassis was designed around the hardware that needed to fit inside it, not just the external dimensions of the robot. As the robot was modular, every single piece of the chassis was 3D printed and assembled through bolts. Additionally, every drive motor and dead wheel was bolted through the base plate.

The three wiring-plate and frame layers included PCB slots, access to the ESP boards, switch slots, and holes for connections to pass between levels. Each of the wiring plates were laser cut and engraved of hardboard, while plastic standoffs and nuts were used for pcb mounting. Rocker switches were simply press fit into their gaps.

Methods/Tools Used:

Primary - 3D printing (Chassis) Laser Cutting (Base Plate, Wiring Plates) Assembly - Press fit, Bolts Detailing - Laser Engraving

Elevator Fabrication

The elevator required several custom parts to work with the bicycle chain available in the lab. I measured the chain to design the sprocket pitch, tooth geometry, and engagement profile, then had the sprockets waterjet-cut. I also machined fourteen shaft collars on the lathe and drill press to secure the sprockets to their shafts.

For the chain-to-claw interface, I adapted the original chain-link shape by adding an external plate and mounting holes while preserving the dimensions needed for chain engagement. I then had to machine and hammer new axle pins into the chain interface to allow it to integrate into the chain properly.

Methods/Tools Used:

Primary - Machining (Waterjet, Grinding, Lathe, Drill Press) Specialized Tools - Chain Break, Hammer Assembly - Pseudo-Riveting, Collaring

Electrical Integration

I contributed to protoboard assembly and soldering, and took primary responsibility for integrating the electronics with the mechanical systems. This involved organizing PCB placement across the chassis layers, routing motor and limit-switch wiring to the ESP boards, and managing cables around moving mechanisms.

Cable routing needed to leave enough slack for the elevator and Spear to travel without snagging, while keeping connections away from moving parts. I also had to preserve access to the power switches and make the battery connections accessible for disconnection. Treating wiring as part of the mechanical layout from the beginning made the final assembly easier to service and troubleshoot, though I will admit that the modular and multi-level design made wire management extremely difficult to keep orderly.

Methods/Tools Used:

Primary - Soldering Iron Secondary - Soldering Fume Fan Wire Management - Wiring Organizers (Custom Printed, Zip Ties)

Modularity During Integration

The detachable front chassis made a real difference during assembly. Instead of taking apart the whole robot whenever we needed to work on a manipulator, we could remove the front assembly and work on it separately. This let us develop the elevator and lead-screw mechanisms while drivetrain tuning continued independently, then reconnect the assemblies for integrated testing.

04 — Engineering Process

Testing & Iteration

The first CAD model was never going to capture every clearance, friction point, or integration issue. As we assembled and tested the robot, I used what we observed to refine component dimensions, improve mechanical movement, and make the subsystems work together more reliably.

Some of the most useful iterations came from relatively small changes: adjusting a sliding fit, refining a custom mount, or changing how a component was retained. Those details made a real difference to whether a mechanism moved consistently once installed in the robot.

1 / 3

Refining Mechanical Performance

Deadwheel Fit and Positioning

The deadwheel system required careful alignment between the axle, bearings, encoder magnet, and custom mounting components. After two iterations of simple refinements like changing axle tolerances and magnetic encoder orientation/positioning, it worked practically flawlessly. Its independent measurements allowed us to navigate by defined coordinates, while avoiding the speed limitations we faced with PID tuning and tape following on our relatively heavy robot.

Lead-Screw Carriage Clearances

Our initial Spear-carriage fit was too tight, causing the slider to bind. I adjusted the dimensions to provide more clearance while still constraining rotation and maintaining alignment with the lead screw. It was a good reminder that a part can look correct in CAD and still need tolerance adjustments once fabricated and installed alongside real hardware.

Repeatable Tower Assembly

The Spear needed to return to a consistent pickup and assembly position so tower pieces could be collected, stacked, and transferred without repeatedly realigning the robot. We tested and iterated on the spear geometry and assembly sequence to improve repeatability. For example, our original centring crescent was actually offset to the side of the spear, which sacraficed majorly on its structural integreity and alignment. Through testing, we came to the conclusions that led us to placing it concentric with the spear.

System Integration and Competition Readiness

We tested subsystems separately where possible, then brought them together within the modular chassis. This allowed mechanical development and drivetrain tuning to proceed in parallel. However, integration was also where our limited development time became apparent: a power surge during tower-sequence calibration on the night before competition prevented us from fully incorporating the complete tower-assembly routine into the competition program.

Although the tower mechanism itself was functional, the experience reinforced how important it is to leave enough time for full-system testing, electrical fault isolation, and validation of the final competition sequence—not just individual mechanisms working as intended.

05 — Outcome

Results & Takeaways

T.A.M.K. brought together a compact modular chassis, four-wheel Mecanum drivetrain, independent deadwheel positioning, a lead-screw tower mechanism, and a chain-lift elevator. My main contribution was turning the mechanical requirements into systems that could be fabricated, assembled, and integrated into one robot.

One of the most successful outcomes was the deadwheel positioning system. After two iterations, it worked practically flawlessly and allowed us to navigate using coordinates rather than relying on continuous tape following. The modular chassis also made it easier for our team to develop and test different subsystems in parallel.

We did not fully integrate the radio-tower assembly sequence into the competition program after a power surge during final calibration. While disappointing, this reinforced the importance of leaving time for complete system integration and testing alongside mechanical development. Despite this, we placed 5th place, and gained significant knowledge in systems design, fabrication, and integration for future projects.

Key takeaway: I gained practical experience taking mechanical designs from CAD through fabrication, machining, assembly, testing, and iteration, while balancing individual component performance with the constraints of a complete robotic system.
