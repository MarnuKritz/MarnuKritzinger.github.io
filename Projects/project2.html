Project 02

Mechanical On/Off Magnet

Originally starting as a subdrone mount for UBC UAS' side VTOL project, I designed a permanent magnet that could be turned on and off through simple rotation. This project involved research, design, iteration, and testing, and is now in its final testing stages.

My role: Designer/Lead

Project period: January 2026 - Present

Team: Individual Project

The Concept

How It Works

The idea behind my design was simple: instead of using electricity to switch a magnet on and off, I wanted to mechanically redirect its magnetic flux by rotating the magnets inside a compact casing. This allows the magnet to hold the subdrone without requiring continuous electrical power, and allows it to release the subdrone extremely easily.

  • On: The magnets are positioned such that the like-metal core polarities (+ on +) align, directing most of the magnetic flux through the external holding surface, allowing the magnet to attach to the subdrone.
  • Off: Rotating the mechanism changes the matal core alignment to be opposing (+ on -), redirecting more of the flux through the internal steel core connection and reducing the external holding force.

The mechanism is designed to be actuated through a simple servo rotation, providing a lightweight alternative to an electrically switched magnetic system.

01 - Engineering Process

Problem & Objectives

In January 2026, I was tasked with working on UAS' side project: A gass-powered Mothership VTOL drone. The goal of this drone was to house/carry a smaller subdrone on the back of the fuselage designed to collect samples or data from natural environments.

the VTOL would then be utilized by companies that needed to collect samples from different ecosystems to fly prolonged distances to these ecosystems, land, release the subdrone to collect samples, then collect the subdrone again and return with the samples.

1 / 2

Objectives and Constraints

The challenge for me? Designing a reliable mounting system that could guide the subdrone into place even if the landing wasn't 100% accurate, securely hold it during flight, and fit within the confined space of the fuselage.

After researching electro-mechanical switch magnets, magnetic chucks, mechanical landing locks, and other options, and consulting my subteam lead, we settled on magnetic force to hold the drone in place. Reliability was a major consideration. Mechanical locking systems can introduce movement or play, so I wanted to explore an alternative. My goal was to develop a magnetic system that could maintain a strong connection to the subdrone without requiring electrical power to sustain the connection.

02 - Engineering Process

Research and Concept Generation

For this project, I did extensive research at a wide variety of magnetic and electromagnetic topics, aided by my Electromagnetism II class at the time. This research into magnetic systems that sustain force without sustained electrical power led me down two possible routes, Magnetic Chucks, and Electro-permanent Magnets. Both paths came with its own challenges and considerations, leading me to do a sufficient amount of research into each option, as described below.

1 / 2

Magnetic Chuck Research

The first option I explored was a permanent magnetic chuck, where rotating a lever reorients magnets inside the casing to redirect magnetic flux and switch the holding force. This approach had several benefits, but also some drawbacks for my design.

Advantages

  • No continuous electrical power required
  • Strong magnetic holding force
  • Flux can be redirected to reduce external magnetic fields

Disadvantages

  • Turning mechanism can require substantial force
  • Can be costly
  • Can be too heavy for weight-sensitive applications

Although this option showed promise, the weight of a complete magnetic chuck was a concern. The Mothership VTOL had a strict weight limit because it needed to carry both the subdrone and a gas engine, making a lightweight design essential.

Electro-Permanent Magnet Research

The second option I explored was an electro-permanent magnet (EPM). Depending on the design, an EPM uses an electrical current pulse to switch its magnetic state, allowing it to maintain its holding force without continuous power. I liked this approach because electrical power was only needed to switch its state, rather than sustain the hold.

Advantages

  • No continuous electrical power required to hold
  • Compact design
  • Strong magnetic holding force

Disadvantages

  • Switching can require high current for strong magnets
  • Can be costly to purchase
  • Can be difficult to source
  • Requires consideration of complex magnetic properties
  • Electrical component failure could affect switching

As I hadn't heard of EPMs before, I really liked this idea. However, the disadvantages outweighed the benefits for my particular application. Still, the research ended up inspiring my final concept when I combined the idea of controlling magnetic flux with the mechanical approach of a magnetic chuck.

Final Concept - Mechanical On/Off Magnet

In the Wikipedia article on EPMs, I came across the image shown here to explain how they work. It captures a similar principle to a magnetic chuck, where permanent magnets are arranged to redirect magnetic flux, while illustrating how an EPM can control its external magnetic field to release its hold.

Looking at this image made me think, "What if, rather than reorienting the magnets inside a magnetic chuck, I moved them to redirect their fields?" From this thought, I made a rough sketch on paper of what I was looking for, then found some similar concepts online.

From there, I refined the design to address issues I identified in those concepts and created an initial CAD prototype of my Mechanical On/Off Magnet. It offered many of the benefits of the alternatives without as many of their disadvantages.

Advantages

  • No continuous electrical power required to hold
  • Compact design
  • Lightweight plastic casing
  • Can be controlled through simple servo rotation
  • Can be manufactured in-house

Disadvantages

  • Required further testing to determine the best magnets to use
  • More time-consuming to develop than purchasing an existing solution

This concept seemed much more promising, as it met our weight requirements while addressing the technical requirements of the project.

03 - Engineering Process

Fabrication & Operation

For the first prototype, I designed the mechanism to rotate through a limited range of 60 degrees. I created guide slots for bolts with locknuts to restrict the motion and used a central M10 bolt as the rotation axle.

I then designed six square openings through the two shells and circular holes to hold the permanent magnets. Their polarities had to be arranged carefully so that rotating the mechanism would change the magnetic flux path between the two operating positions.

04 - Engineering Process

Testing & Iteration

Unfortunately, I found that this first model did not support nearly strong enough magnets to pick up even the most minor of objects. Additionally, I had originally chosen cold-rolled steel for the metal cores of the prototype, as I had found others online using it for similar applications. Through this testing, I found it to not nearly be permeable enough, and found that I had to make a few alterations for my next prototype.

Namely, I made the next prototype much bigger. This would allow me to support much larger magnets, and conduct testing with them to find the optimal metal core material to use. Additionally, after further research, I have decided to try and use soft-iron cores for the next prototype, as it has a much greater permeability than that of the steel I had previously used.

1 / 2

Testing and Improvements

As can be seen through the images, I have made a variety of upgrades to the new version of the magnet.

Design Iterations

  • New Range of Motion - I changed the range of motion to be 180 degrees as opposed to 60, resulting in the magnet needing 2 larger cores rather 6. This allows for one larger magnet to be used as opposed to various smaller ones.
  • Internal Sliding Slot - I changed the slot to be encapsulated within the casing, preventing it from becoming stuck on external piecs or jamming into fingers while rotating.
  • Rotation Axis Bolt - I made the rotating bolt in the centre smaller and able to freely slide within one of the shells, allowing for smoother rotation as opposed to the fitted hex designs on the previous prototype.

05 - Outcome

NEXT STEPS

The next steps for this project is to order the magnets I designed it for off of amazon, then machine the soft iron cores to fit inside of it. Following this, further testing will likely be necessary before a final iteration is made, then I will potentially attempt to scale it down to a more compact design.

Key outcome: Developed a lightweight, mechanically switched permanent magnet concept that can be actuated through simple servo rotation, providing a potential solution for securely carrying and releasing a subdrone without continuous electrical power.
